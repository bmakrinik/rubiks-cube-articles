# Rubik's Cube

*Why most marketplaces do the right thing at the wrong stage, and how to avoid it*


**Published:** 2026-02-23  
**Source:** [https://bmak.substack.com/p/the-marketplace-scaling-ladder-framework](https://bmak.substack.com/p/the-marketplace-scaling-ladder-framework)

---

Teams build beautiful, curated supply with hand-picked sellers and perfect quality controls. And then they watch a scrappier competitor with worse quality but 10x the volume lock up the market. The teams weren’t doing the wrong things. They were doing the right things at the wrong time. Marketplace growth follows a predictable pattern. The instincts that got you through one stage will actively sabotage you in the next.

Plenty has been written about marketplace scaling. [Lenny Rachitsky’s series](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-kickstart-and-scale-a-marketplace) covers cold start tactics. [NFX’s Pete Flint](https://www.nfx.com/post/marketplace-expansion-framework) breaks down supply vs demand constraints. [Sharetribe](https://www.sharetribe.com/academy/how-to-scale-your-marketplace/) and others detail geographic expansion playbooks. These focus on what to scale (geography, category, segment) rather than how to make the hard operational decisions that determine whether you actually capture value.

**This framework is different.** It’s about the counterintuitive moves operators need to make. Marketplace growth isn’t one continuous journey. It’s distinct stages, each requiring strategies that would destroy you in the previous stage[1](#footnote-1).

## **Stage 1: Ignition - Solve the Chicken & Egg**

Start with the scarce side. And [most of the time](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-kickstart-and-scale-a-marketplace), that’s supply. In almost every marketplace, quality supply is the bottleneck. Demand is relatively abundant; they’ll use whoever solves their problem.

So you start with supply. Get 10-20 high-quality suppliers who can actually deliver. Don’t worry about perfect coverage. Worry about proving value.

What does success at this stage actually look like? **Three to five sellers generating meaningful revenue (enough that they’d be upset if you shut down), repeat buyers even at modest rates, and, counterintuitively, some complaints.** Complaints are good. They mean people care enough to tell you what’s broken.

You know you’re ready for Stage 2 when you’ve stopped asking “will anyone use this?” and started asking “how do we do more of this?”

## **Stage 2: Expansion - Scale Before It Breaks**

This is the stage where operators’ instincts betray them. You’ve worked so hard to curate quality in Stage 1. Every supplier was hand-selected. Every transaction felt important. Now you’re opening the floodgates, and it feels wrong.

> **If quality doesn’t drop during expansion, you’re not really scaling.**

When you go from 20 suppliers to 200, or from 2 cities to 20, you cannot maintain the same quality bar. You’re moving faster than your quality controls can scale. That’s exactly the point.

Airbnb’s early scaling tells this story well. Jonathan Golden, who joined Airbnb in 2011 and watched it [scale](https://medium.com/@jgolden/lessons-learned-scaling-airbnb-100x-b862364fb3a7) 100x, described how the team was ramping up 50 markets simultaneously at one point and clearly spread too thin. They scaled back to a maximum of ten markets at a time, but they kept pushing. The early Airbnb experience was inconsistent by design. Some listings were incredible, others were terrible. Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia personally photographed host listings and met every host in New York, but that obviously couldn’t last at scale. They accepted the quality variance because the alternative (staying small and curated) would have left the market to someone else.

In Stage 2, your job is to grow GBV 10-100x while monitoring but not yet optimizing quality metrics. You’re building the denominator. You need transaction volume, geographic coverage, category depth. You’ll see higher cancellation rates, more customer complaints, supply that doesn’t meet your standards, inconsistent experiences across markets.

Tolerate it. Document it. Don’t try to fix it (yet).

Marketplaces stall at six to low seven digit GBV because they tried to maintain boutique quality. They rejected marginal supply, over-indexed on NPS, and throttled growth to “protect the brand.”

The failure mode here is celebrating “quality” metrics while growth stagnates. Your best suppliers are maxed out and you haven’t built depth. You’re afraid to expand into new markets because “we haven’t perfected the current ones.”

You’ll know you’re stuck in Stage 2 when you’re celebrating “quality” metrics while growth stagnates, your best suppliers are maxed out, and you’re afraid to expand because “we haven’t perfected the current markets”.

## **Stage 3: Curation - The Painful Decisions**

By now you’ve scaled. You have volume. You have coverage. And you have a quality problem that’s starting to hurt. Buyer retention is slipping. NPS is trending down.

Airbnb hit this inflection point and acted on it decisively. Since launching its updated hosting quality [system](https://news.airbnb.com/airbnb-global-quality-report/), the company has removed hundres of thoussand of listings that failed to meet quality standards. That’s not a small number; it’s a meaningful chunk of supply, deliberately removed to protect the marketplace’s long-term health. The result? Customer service issue rates decreased, guest NPS improved, and host cancellations dropped.

> Are you willing to remove revenue-generating supply? If the answer is no, you’ll never leave Stage 2.

The work at this stage is systematic.

- First, define explicit standards: not vague “quality” but measurable thresholds for cancellation rates, customer ratings, response times, stockout rates.
- Second, make those standards visible to the demand side through ratings, reliability scores, and verified badges. Transparency forces accountability.
- Third, enforce ruthlessly.

I know what you’re thinking: “They’re contributing to GMV. Removing them hurts our numbers.” It does, in the short term. Here’s what happens if you don’t: your best suppliers leave, your highest-value customers churn, your marketplace becomes a race to the bottom. You’re optimizing for GMV that has no margin and no moat.

You don’t delete supply overnight. You create systems that push quality up naturally. Monetary penalties for cancellations and quality violations make bad behavior expensive while keeping the demand side whole. De-ranking poor performers in search results lets them churn naturally. Graduated consequences (warning, temporary suspension, removal) give suppliers a chance to improve.

**You’re successfully executing Stage 3 when you can point to specific suppliers you removed despite their revenue contribution.** If you can’t name them, you’re not really enforcing standards.

## **Stage 4: Optimization - The Profitability Unlock**

This is where you actually make money. And the biggest missed opportunity is take rates. Here’s a contrarian truth that most marketplace operators refuse to believe: **most of your supply won’t churn when you raise take rates**. Some marketplaces agonize over 1-2% take rate increases for months. They run endless surveys, model worst-case churn scenarios, and delay because leadership is terrified of supply backlash.

The economics explain why. Supply doesn’t actually absorb take rate increases; they pass 70-80% of it through to demand. The math is asymmetric. A 20% take rate increase only needs to maintain 83% of transaction volume to be revenue positive. In practice, volume drops are much smaller. What do you need to believe to green-light this? That fewer than 17% of your transactions will disappear. If you’ve done the work in Stages 1-3, that’s a very safe bet.

When should you do it? When you have several months of proven liquidity, you’re past the initial growth phase, supply has limited viable alternatives, and demand NPS is stable. Start in mature markets first (more liquidity means more pricing power) and watch supply behavior rather than supply surveys.

### Beyond Take Rates: Value-Add Services

Once you have scale, the second profitability lever is offering services that make your supply more successful. Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA) is the canonical example. They went from connecting buyers and sellers to handling warehousing, shipping, and returns. This created massive additional revenue while increasing supply stickiness. Identify the operational pain points your supply faces, then build tools or services to solve them. I have written more about this on [Another approach to the chicken and egg problem](https://bmak.substack.com/p/how-did-amazon-become-a-marketplace)

> NYU Stern professor [Sonia Marciano](https://vimeo.com/253402779) has introduced the idea of focusing on the piece of the supply chain with the greatest discrepancy. Amazon quickly realized that the greatest variance in the customer experience was the fulfillment. To mitigate, Amazon launched the biggest game changer in their marketplace play, the **Fulfillment By Amazon** (FBA) program. FBA allows sellers to send their inventory in Amazon Fulfillment Centers. Amazon stores seller inventory and owns the fulfillment when the seller receives an order. Amazon “[will pick, pack, ship, and provide customer service](https://www.amazon.com/fulfillment-by-amazon/b?ie=UTF8&node=13245485011)”. With FBA, Amazon increased control of the fulfillment experience, reducing out of stock (as the product is already in an Amazon warehouse), and ensuring on time delivery. At the same time, Amazon increased utilization of their fulfillment network which justified higher investments and growth.
>
> Amazon provided incentives for using the program as it awarded the Prime badge to third party products fulfilled through the Amazon Fulfillment Network, instantly making third party products more compelling and increasing conversion. This made FBA more compelling, driving high seller adoption, and the combination was another tailwind in the marketplace flywheel.

**Examples of value-add services:**

- **Fulfillment & logistics**: Handle shipping, warehousing, or delivery
- **Financial services**: Offer working capital loans, payment processing, or insurance
- **Marketing & advertising**: Let supply pay to be featured or promoted
- **Analytics & tools**: Provide dashboards, inventory management, or yield optimization
- **Professional services**: Offer photography, copywriting, or quality certification

Done right, value-add services increase both your take rate and supply retention.

## **The Pattern That Matters**

Marketplaces don’t fail because they don’t know what to do. They fail because they do the right thing at the wrong stage.

Optimizing for quality in Stage 1 means you never achieve liquidity. Refusing to scale aggressively in Stage 2 means a competitor takes the market. Avoiding hard supply decisions in Stage 3 leads to a race to the bottom. Not capturing value in Stage 4 means unprofitable scale.

Most marketplaces die somewhere between Stage 2 and Stage 3. They scaled successfully but couldn’t make the transition to quality enforcement. The shift from “grow at all costs” to “grow what’s worth keeping” is the hardest gear change in marketplace operations, and most teams can’t make it because it requires them to undo the exact mindset that got them there.

Know your stage. Do what’s uncomfortable but necessary. Then move to the next one.
